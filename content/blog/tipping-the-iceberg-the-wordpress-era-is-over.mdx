On 21 October, the PHP project shipped emergency fixes for two critical flaws, **CVE-2026-109618** and **CVE-2026-109619**. People are calling them PHP2Shell. Both allow unauthenticated remote code execution, so an attacker does not need a login.

**TL;DR:** Patch PHP today. Then ask how much control you have over your content, your hosting and your way out.

If you run WordPress, you run PHP. This one is yours to fix.

## Patch first

**Heads up:** a new CMS will not protect a site that is exposed right now. Follow the CISA guidance before you read anything else: [Critical Vulnerabilities in PHP Require Immediate Patching](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2026/10/21/critical-vulnerabilities-php-require-immediate-patching).

## What you don't control in a crisis

* Do you know who has access to your site, and where it runs?
* Can your team change the hosting without rebuilding the content?
* Can you move your content to another tool if you need to?
* Does a second developer know how to build and deploy the site?

If the answer to any of these is "only our agency knows", an incident is a bad time to find out.

## Your content, in your repo

TinaCMS stores your content as readable files (Markdown, MDX and JSON) in a Git repo that your team controls. An article is a file with a history. You can open it, copy it and reuse it without the original editor.

Editors get visual editing on top. Developers keep control of the components that render the pages.

TinaCMS works with frameworks like Next.js and Astro, so you choose the stack and where it is hosted. You can also [self-host TinaCMS](https://tina.io/docs/self-hosted/overview) if you want to run the backend yourself.

**TinaCloud keeps a database-backed index of your content.** It keeps queries fast. The files in your repo are the source of truth, and the index can be rebuilt from them. That's a useful difference, but it isn't "no database".

TinaCloud's [Editorial Workflow](https://tina.io/docs/tinacloud/editorial-workflow) can also send content changes through branches and draft pull requests, so your team can review them before they go live. This needs a paid plan and some setup.

## An option: deploy static

Control also covers what runs on your public site. With TinaCMS you can choose to build your site as static pages. Visitors then get files from a CDN, and the editing system sits apart from the public site.

**Heads up:** TinaCMS is not static by default. If you want a static site, you build for it on purpose. Dynamic features like forms, search and personalised pages need their own plan.

## Check your exit

Before you decide anything, test how portable your site really is:

1. Ask a second developer to build, edit and deploy the site from the documented access.
2. Open a sample article and its media on a clean clone, without the original editor.
3. Restore a changed page from history, and time how long it takes.
4. List where leads, orders and customer records live. A CMS move changes where content is stored. It doesn't move your business data.

If these are hard, that's worth knowing before the next advisory.

## If you want to try TinaCMS

You don't have to move the whole site. Run a small pilot instead:

1. Pick a representative set of pages, including the awkward ones.
2. Convert the content and media. Our [WordPress to TinaCMS guide](https://tina.io/docs/guides/converting-wordpress-to-tina) shows the path.
3. Keep your URLs, and map redirects for any that change.
4. Have a real editor do real tasks.
5. Check what breaks.

Then decide with evidence.

## The takeaway

PHP2Shell is a patch job today. It's also a good moment to ask how much control you have over your website.
